This study aims to address two major challenges in the digital restoration of murals: defect segmentation and large-scale defect repair. We propose a novel solution, combining the SAM- Adapter segmentation method with semi-supervised training and a Text-to-Inpainting strategy. In the aspect of defect segmentation, by introducing thresholding and semi-supervised training, we effectively enhanced the precision and applicability in handling sparse datasets. For large-scale defect repair, this study innovatively applies BPO (Black-box Prompt Optimization) technology, combined with GPT4V (ision) and Stable Diffusion, to achieve precise reconstruction of murals’ complex textures and structures through text-guided methods. Through extensive experimental validation, our method has demonstrated signiﬁcant effectiveness in defect segmentation and large-scale repair, providing new perspectives and tools for the digital preservation of murals, and is of great signiﬁcance for the protection of cultural heritage.

Murals are an invaluable record of human history and culture, encapsu- lating diverse artistic styles and reﬂecting societal transformations across eras. Yet these fragile works steadily deteriorate under the combined impact of moisture ﬂuctuations, light exposure, and human activity. Digital restoration now provides a non-invasive, high-ﬁdelity route to conserve such paintings, safeguarding their aesthetic and historical value for future generations. Two technical bottlenecks, however, still limit practical deploy- ment. First, damage segmentation is hampered by complex brush- strokes, irregular textures, and-crucially-the scarcity of annotated mural-damage datasets. Second, large-area inpainting remains difﬁ- cult because severely eroded regions supply few visual cues; even state- of-the-art diffusion inpainting1 and LAMA2 degrade when the missing region is extensive. Related work. Traditional studies coupled conservators’ craft knowl- edge with digital pipelines: early efforts integrated manual practice and imagingsoftware3; morphological component analysis wasapplied to Tang- dynasty tomb paintings4; texture-synthesis and curvature-diffusion meth- ods were explored for Dunhuang murals5,6; non-texture techniques addressed Maijishan grottoes7; and Criminisi-style patch ﬁlling was reﬁned for Dunhuang8 and Wutai9 murals, then further optimized10. Deep learning expanded these capabilities: neural networks11, GANs12, adaptive local convolutions13, edge-guided dual-stage restorers14 and diffusion models for Dunhuang15,16 all improved quality, yet still struggled with heterogeneous styles or very large lacunae.

In parallel, image segmentation advanced rapidly. The Segment Anything Model (SAM)17 and its medical derivatives18,19 offer strong cross- task generalization; SAM-Adapter20 boosts performance via task-adaptive layers.TransformerdesignssuchasMISSFormer21and TransCeption22,and semi-supervised frameworks like LViT23, In bridge structural-health mon- itoring, Bernardini et al. propose a DSF/DEI-based methodology that ﬁrst achieves a robust healthy-vs-damaged separation under scarce labels24, pushed accuracy further-yet mural damage segmentation remains chal- lenging because most methods rely on abundant labels25. Image-inpainting research ranges from PDE-based ﬁlling26 and exemplar synthesis27,28 to GAN or transformer variants. Siamese expansion networks29, memory-augmented restorers30,31, global-local completion32,33, partial or gated convolutions34,35, high-resolution patch synthesis35,36, edge- guided pipelines37, multi-modality guidance38,39 and wavelet self-attention GANs40 each target structural or textural realism, but often suffer style drift or local artefacts in very large holes. Meanwhile, multimodal learning and prompt optimization have matured. Advances include multi-task prompt frameworks (TRIPLET 41), robustness-oriented bottlenecks42, knowledge-guided storytelling43, cross- lingual retrieval44, hallucination suppression in LVLMs45 and two-stage visual-text translators46. Methods This chapter provides a detailed introduction to our proposed mural restoration method. The overall framework of our approach is illustrated in Fig. 1 and can be divided into two key stages: (1) precise segmentation of damaged areas and (2) large-scale restoration guided by prompt optimization. First, the accurate segmentation of damaged areas serves as the foun- dation of the entire mural restoration task. We employ a semi-supervised SAM-Adapter model, which is trained by leveraging both annotated and unannotated data to effectively address data scarcity issues. During training,

a thresholding strategy is used to enhance the reliability of the model’s predictions, ensuring high-precision segmentation masks. Second, we ﬁne-tune the Stable Diffusion model using LoRA to enable accurate inﬁlling of mural-speciﬁc artistic styles and textures. Additionally, weutilizeGPT-4VtogeneratepromptsforStableDiffusion.Toensurehigh- quality content generation, we further optimize the prompts using Black- box Prompt Optimization (BPO) techniques. In the following sections, we will provide a detailed explanation of the semi-supervised SAM-Adapter segmentation and the large-scale text-gui- ded mural restoration process.

Text-to-inpainting for large hole mural restoration In the ﬁeld of mural restoration, the challenge of handling large-scale damage is particularly daunting. Traditional inpainting techniques often have limited effectiveness in these scenarios, as they struggle to accurately reproduce the complex artistic styles and textures found in murals. To address this issue, we have combined the LoRA-trained Stable Diffusion model with the GPT4V (ision)-assisted BPO technology to achieve more precise and culturally sensitive mural restoration. LoRA Training of Stable Diffusion: In the process of training the Stable Diffusion model using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), we have adopted an innovative approach to enhance the model’s performance, particularly in the ﬁeld of art and cultural heritage preservation, such as mural restoration. The crux of this process lies in the utilization of LoRA technology, a highly efﬁcientmethodforﬁne-tuninglargemodels,whichoptimizesperformance rapidly and effectively without compromising the model’s complexity and expressiveness. The following sections provide a detailed description of how we implemented this technology: • Fine-tuning Process: During the ﬁne-tuning of the Stable Diffusion model with LoRA technology, we integrated a relatively small LoRA model ﬁle (ranging from 1 MB to 200 MB) with the existing Stable Diffusion checkpoint models. This integration enabled us to introduce newthematic and stylistic concepts into the model, speciﬁcally tailored for the ﬁeld of muralrestoration.Sucha combination hasenhanced the model’s capability to generate images in line with these new concepts, making it more apt for this specialized application context. • LoRA Technology: Developed by researchers at Microsoft, LoRA technology represents a novel approach for efﬁciently ﬁne-tuning large language models. Its primary advantage is the ability to ﬁne-tune large models without the need for full retraining, thus saving considerable time and computational resources. In the ﬁne-tuning process of the Stable Diffusion model, the application of LoRA technology not only improved the model’s ability to accurately maintain the original style and texture of damaged murals but also its efﬁciency and user- friendliness allowed for the model to be easily customized to meet speciﬁc creative objectives and needs, thereby reducing the burden of technology and resources.

This LoRA training methodology provides an effective and user- friendly solution for performance optimization of the Stable Diffusion model in speciﬁc application areas such as mural restoration, signiﬁcantly enhancing the model’s performance and adaptability. Results In this section, we present the rationale for our experiments, aimed at demonstratingtheeffectivenessofour digitalmuralrestorationmethod.We began by comparing our blind restoration technique, integrating segmen- tation and inpainting, against other methods. In the segmentation phase,we evaluated our unique semi-supervised training strategy, including an abla- tion study for deeper insights. For the inpainting component, we ﬁrst conducted comparative experiments against other inpainting methods without the use of prompt engineering. This was followed by further experiments to assess the impact of incorporating prompt engineering. These sequential steps were pivotal in showcasing the comprehensive capabilitiesofourmethod,especiallyinthenuancedﬁeldofculturalheritage preservation.

Dataset and experimental setup The mural dataset used in this experiment is based on our team’s previous work56 and encompasses mural images from various historical periods and grottoes in Dunhuang, ensuring diversity and representativeness. This dataset includes murals of different artistic styles, reﬂecting the evolution of mural art from the Northern Dynasties, Sui-Tang, to the Song-Yuan peri- ods. Therefore, it provides a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating the applicability of our restoration method across different mural styles. Additionally, the dataset has been standardized, with all mural images resized to a uniform resolution to ensure experimental consistency and comparability. The segmentation dataset consists of 1000 mural images, which are divided into a training set and a test set in an 8: 2 ratio. To simulate the real- world challenge of limited labeled data, 50% of the training images are annotated, while the remaining 50% are unlabeled. This setup closely aligns with practical application scenarios and allows us to assess the model’s performance in a semi-supervised learning environment. Quality labeling for prompt-optimization pairs. To construct training pairs for the prompt optimization model, quality labels were assigned via a two-stage protocol rather than purely automatic thresh- olds. First, under a ﬁxed mask ratio (30-50%) and identical generation settings per image, we computed PSNR/SSIM, performed z-score nor- malization within image groups, and assigned initial labels by quantiles (top-q →HQS, bottom-q →LQS), discarding the middle ≈40% uncertain band to avoid boundary noise. Second, two annotators with Dunhuang-mural background audited a concise perceptual checklist (boundary continuity, tone/material consistency, no new iconographic elements, minimal change) to correct or remove cases where metrics and perception diverged; samples with annotator disagreement were dis- carded. The cleaned set comprised 2000 samples (1600 train/400 vali- dation) used to train the Llama2-7B prompt optimizer. All experiments were conducted on 4xA100 GPUs to ensure compu- tational efﬁciency and stability for large-scale mural restoration tasks. Running experiments and tests on this dataset enables a thorough evalua- tion of the model’s adaptability across various mural styles. Furthermore, by

comparing different training strategies, we validate the proposed method’s performance under varying levels of data availability. LoRA ﬁne-tuning details. Comparative analysis of blind restoration methods The main goal of this experiment was to assess and compare our proposed automated mural blind restoration method with existing technologies in handling mural damage. Through this assessment, we validated the effec- tiveness of our method and explored its potential application in complex cultural heritage preservation tasks. We examined three methods: the IA method57, which combined the SAM segmentation algorithm and Lama inpainting technology for detecting and ﬁlling damaged areas; the Bring Old58 method, which used Unet segmentation, suitable for general image processing tasks; and our method, which combined advanced image seg- mentation techniques and a text-based inpainting strategy, aiming to more accurately understand and repair damage on murals. In the implementation process, each method ﬁrst identiﬁed and seg- mented the damaged areas of the mural images, a prerequisite for successful restoration. Subsequently, their respective inpainting techniques were applied to repair these areas. During the restoration process, we particularly focused on whetherthe methodscould preserve the original style and details of the murals. The restored images were jointly evaluated by art restoration experts and image processing specialists, with evaluation criteria including the consistency of the repaired area with the original image, the degree of detail preservation, and the overall aesthetic effect. Although the IA method performed well in general image processing tasks, it had limitations in speciﬁc mural damage recognition, especially when dealing with subtle damages close to the background colors. The Unet segmentation of the Bring Old method worked well for obvious damages, butitsprecisiondecreasedwhendealingwithcomplexorirregulardamages, and sometimes it produced stylistically inconsistent results. In contrast, our method showed higher accuracy in the damage recognition stage, especially in identifying subtle damages, and was able to maintain the style and details of the original murals during the repair process, thereby receiving high praise from experts in terms of artistic integrity. These experimental results emphasized that in cultural heritage pre- servation, especially in complex and precious mural restoration tasks, tra- ditional image processing methods may not meet the high standards of restoration needs. Our method, by combining advanced segmentation techniques and a text-based inpainting strategy, demonstrated outstanding capabilities in handling complex mural damages.

Discussion This study presents an integrated pipeline that tackles two long-standing obstacles in digital mural restoration-damage segmentation and large-area inpainting-by coupling a semi-supervised SAM-Adapter network with a BPO-optimized, GPT-4V-guided Stable Diffusion framework. Extensive experiments across multiple mural styles conﬁrm that the proposed approach delivers higher mask accuracy under limited labels and markedly better visual ﬁdelity when reconstructing wide lacunae, thus offering a practical route for large-scale heritage digitization. Despite these advances, several issues merit further investigation. Data scarcity and diversity remain a fundamental constraint: the model’s per- formance is still bounded by the quality and quantity of available annota- tions, and its accuracy drops when confronted with extremely rare or idiosyncraticstyles.Computationalaccessibilityisanotherconcern,Undera typical single-image setting (512 × 512, batch = 1) on an A100-40GB with mixedprecision—usingSAM-Adapter(ViT-H),Llama-2-7B,alocalLoRA- tuned Stable Diffusion with 30 steps, and one GPT-4V expansion—the end- to-end latency is ≈6.2 s (with ≈5.0 s local compute). The dominant bot- tleneck is the diffusion denoising loop (~78% of local time), followed by VAE encode/decode (~10%). As the current pipeline relies on high-capacity visual-language models and diffusion generators; deploying such resources may be prohibitive for some conservation institutions. Aesthetic integrity alsoposesanopenchallenge-fullyrespectingtheoriginalartisticintentwhile performing algorithmic reconstructions requires deeper collaboration between technologists and art historians. Finally, although our method handles large gaps better than prior work, cases of extreme loss, where virtually no contextual cues survive, continue to test the limits of automated inpainting.
